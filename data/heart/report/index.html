K-Ont vs OnT on heart disease

Does embedding a knowledge graph with K-Ont help a heart-disease classifier more than standard OnT? 303 patients (UCI Cleveland), the heart ontology and its knowledge graph, 5 classifiers, 10 cross-validation folds. The diagnosis is never shown to OnT or K-Ont.

K-Ont vs OnT as a patient representation (AUC, mean over 5 models) — — of 5 models significantly better (p < 0.05)
Adding K-Ont to the full table (AUC change, mean over 5 models) — Raw + K-Ont vs raw table, all labels
With 10% of the labels (AUC change, mean over 5 models) — Raw + K-Ont vs raw table

What was compared

Standard OnT

Trained on the heart ontology's 32 classes only, with OnT's own data preparation. It has no place for individual patients, so a patient is represented by the OnT vectors of its five categorical values (gender, chest pain type, resting ECG, ST slope, thallium scan).

K-Ont

OnT extended to the knowledge graph: the ontology plus the training patients as individuals. Each patient is one short sentence of all 13 facts, typed under "patient" and linked by the five relations to its value concepts. Retrained inside every fold on that fold's training patients; test patients are only encoded.

Controls and models

The raw 13-column table alone; the plain, untrained encoder reading the same patient sentence; logistic regression, random forest, SVM, gradient boosting and a small neural network (MLP). Embeddings are reduced to 16 numbers per patient (PCA) before the classifiers see them. The diagnosis link is removed from everything the encoders see.

One patient, two ways

Pick a patient. OnT sees five separate concepts; K-Ont turns the whole record into one vector and finds the patient's nearest neighbours by it.

The classifiers learn from 10, 20, 50 or 100% of the training patients' labels (3 random subsets per fold). The embeddings come from all training patients without labels, which is fair because no encoder ever sees a diagnosis.

How to read this

    

      
    
        
  • K-Ont is a better representation of patients than standard OnT: it places every patient, including unseen ones, under the ontology's "patient" class, keeps every patient distinct, and its vectors predict the diagnosis better than OnT's on their own.
    • 
        
  • When the classifier already has the complete table and enough labels, no embedding adds anything: the table holds all the information the knowledge graph has, and this ontology states no medical knowledge (risk factors, causes) beyond the columns.
    • 
        
  • K-Ont's own training is not what makes the difference here: the plain encoder reading the same sentence scores about the same. The gain over OnT comes from embedding the whole patient rather than separate concepts.
    • 
        
  • With few labels the embeddings hurt rather than help: 16 extra numbers per patient on 24 to 48 labelled patients let the classifiers overfit. Pretrained vectors help with few labels only when they carry information the few labels cannot provide, and here they repeat the table.
    • 
        
  • When columns are missing from the table, the knowledge-graph embedding brings their information back: with chest pain, thallium and vessels hidden, adding K-Ont lifts every model significantly. It edges OnT because OnT can only hold categorical concepts, not the number of vessels. The plain encoder reading the same sentence does as well, so the benefit comes from embedding each patient's facts, which needs individual embedding as K-Ont does, not from K-Ont's extra training.
    • 
        
  • So a knowledge-graph embedding pays off where the graph holds something the classifier's own input does not. For a stronger case: a graph with real domain knowledge (risk factors, drugs, conditions, links between patients), and a K-Ont training signal tied to it.
